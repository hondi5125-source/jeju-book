A chapter from Jeju: What Nature Began, People Shaped

A Word Called Gwendang

Yang Yong-ho

In Jeju, people rarely know you by your name alone. They ask whose son you are and which village you come from, and somewhere in the answer they find someone you both know. There is a Jeju word for that web of people.

Whose Son Are You?

My name only made sense to people once it came after my father’s.

“Where are you from?”

When I answered “Wasan,” the questions moved on. What was my surname? Who was my father?

After a few exchanges, someone we both knew would turn up.

“Ah, so you’re that family’s son.”

Only then did the other person’s face relax a little.

As a child I found these conversations strange. Why not simply get to know the person in front of you? Why go back to his father and grandfather, even the village he was born in?

In Jeju, other people always stood behind any one person.

Those ties were called gwendang.

1 gwendang The Jeju word for one’s relatives near and far, by blood and by marriage. Hyeon Pyeong-hyo’s Jeju Dialect Dictionary links it to the Sino-Korean term gwondang, “the circle of those one looks after.”

Strictly, gwendang meant relatives by blood and by marriage, but degrees of kinship could not explain it all. An elder whose face you had seen in the neighborhood for years was a samchun, and the owner of the restaurant you often went to was a samchun too.

2 samchun Literally “uncle,” but used in Jeju as a term of affection and respect for any older person of one’s village or kin, man or woman.

“Samchun, what’s good today?”

Even without knowing someone’s name, calling them samchun opened the conversation.

Not everyone you called that was gwendang. But within that one form of address, people joined by blood and people grown close through living side by side were not strictly separated.

They were people who would find it hard to turn away when I was in trouble, and for whom I, too, would get up and go when something happened in their house.

Relationships did not exist apart from daily life.

They were woven into the business of living.

I Knew the Faces but Not the Names

On the nights of our family’s sikge, a few close gwendang would come. We held the rite, shared the food, and saw one another’s faces.

3 sikge The Jeju word for jesa, the memorial rite held at night on the anniversary of an ancestor’s death, when the family offers food and drink to the ancestor and then shares the meal.

Weddings, funerals, and grave-tending days brought together even more people than a sikge.

The faces of the elders I met there were not unfamiliar. But I often mixed up their names. I did not know exactly whose brother someone was, or how many degrees removed from our family.

I knew the faces but not what to call them.

So sometimes I just bowed first and waited for them to recognize me.

Strangely, they always knew me.

“Aren’t you Geun-hyo’s youngest boy?”

Once my father’s name came up, the conversation quickened. Someone would say whom I took after, and then ask where I lived now.

I did not know their names or how we were related, but they knew whose son I was.

At the time it made me a little uncomfortable.

It felt as if they knew more of my story than I did.

Only as I got older did I understand. They did not know me particularly well as a person. They remembered the people around me.

I was explained through my father, and my father through my grandfather and his brothers.

Follow those ties, and you arrived at a whole family and a whole village.

No One Took Attendance, but Everyone Knew

As Chuseok, Korea’s autumn harvest festival, drew near, the clan gathered and went round the ancestors’ graves.

We cut the grass with sickles. When one grave was done we moved to the next. After lunch we picked up the sickles again.

Some people I saw only once a year.

Yet without anyone counting, everyone knew who had come and who had not.

“So-and-so’s family had several sons come this year.”

“So-and-so hasn’t shown up for years.”

Those who lived on the mainland and could not come sometimes sent money instead. A treasurer was chosen to collect it, and with it we bought food to offer at the graves and food to share among ourselves. No one took attendance, but everyone knew exactly who had come.

When the drink went around, stories not usually spoken rose up over the grass. Old grievances came out and voices rose.

Still, no one put down their sickle and went home.

Even after raised voices, people moved on to the next grave. At lunchtime they sat in the same place and ate together.

Quarreling was not the opposite of gwendang.

Because people remembered the same ancestors and did the same work together, there were things to feel hurt about. If there had been no tie at all, there would have been nothing to argue over, nothing to resent.

As a child I thought the day was only about cutting the grass on the graves.

Looking back, while they cut the grass, people were also checking one another’s faces.

I was born in Wasan, but I went to school in Jeju City. Even though I went back on weekends and holidays, I was not like the children who grew up running through the village lanes every day. I didn’t know which house belonged to which family, and many of the local names given to individual fields were unfamiliar to me.

Yet every time I went home, there were people who knew me better than I knew myself.

I did not know their names, but they knew whose son I was and whose grandson.

Home was the place where I was born.

But it was also the place where there were people who remembered me.

The Ledger Recorded Time as Well

When something big happened in the family, out came the ledger.

People came to weddings, and people came to funerals. The names and amounts on the envelopes were copied one by one into the ledger.

When the event was over, the ledger was not thrown away.

When something happened in another family, it came out again. You checked how much that family had given and decided how much to give this time.

To my young eyes this looked like strange arithmetic.

It seemed as though even congratulations and condolences were being written down as money.

But that money did not change hands once and end there.

If I received today, my turn to give back would come someday. On a day when a family needed a large sum, many small sums from many people gathered in one house, and next time that house helped with another family’s affairs.

It was not only amounts that were written in the ledger.

It also recorded who had come to my side, and to whom I must now go.

Of course, it was not only gratitude that the ledger kept.

When someone did not give back as much as they had received, there was resentment. Perhaps that family had fallen on hard times; perhaps their circumstances had changed since then. But the ledger did not record any of that.

These gifts, called bujo, were a strength that carried families through hard days together.

4 bujo Money given at weddings, funerals, and other major family occasions. The amounts were recorded and later reciprocated, making bujo both a form of mutual aid and a social obligation.

At the same time, they could feel like a debt that must someday be repaid.

Comfort Came Fast, and So Did Gossip

On a small island, you met the same faces again and again.

The person you passed on the way to the fields you saw again at the market, and someone you met at a wedding feast you would meet before long at a house of mourning.

When something good happened in a family, word spread quickly. When something bad happened, it was hard to hide for long.

Comfort came fast.

Gossip came fast too.

At election time, “our gwendang” could be a way of asking for votes. When you had business at a government office, someone might ask whether you knew anyone there.

From outside it could look like favoritism; from inside it could feel like an obligation there was no escaping.

The ties that joined people could sometimes become lines that divided them.

Closeness could be a strength.

It could also be a burden.

Gwendang did not name only good relationships.

It named relationships in which people had watched each other’s happy days and sad days for so long that they could not easily pretend not to see.

The Grass Was Neatly Cut, but…

In the old days, people came to cut the grass.

Now more and more families send a hired company instead. More descendants live on the mainland, and some are too old to climb up to the graves.

The feeling has surely not disappeared.

Only the days on which that feeling was confirmed have grown fewer.

The workers cut the grass cleanly with brush cutters. The cut grass lay neatly and the ground around the graves was tidy.

But there was no way to tell who had grown up that year and who had grown old.

No voices asked whose son had come, or who was showing his face for the first time in years.

There were now people to do the work for us.

They could not stand in for the time spent remembering one another’s faces.

The grass was cut more cleanly than before.

Learning who was connected to whom grew a little harder.

On Sad Days, You Go

Still, when news of a death comes, many people go in person.

Even when it is far, they go.

Some say you may miss the happy occasions, but you must go to the sad ones.

You don’t do anything special at a funeral hall.

You take the chief mourner’s hand and say a few words. Not knowing what to say, you may simply bow your head. You eat, exchange a few words with people you know, and leave.

No one stays for days to help as they once did.

Even so, people make the trip.

Long ago, if someone had asked me what gwendang meant, I would have begun by explaining degrees of kinship.

Now I think I would answer differently.

Rather than weighing how closely we are related, I think I would ask first whether I can go to someone on the day something happens to them.

I was inside those ties from the day I was born.

But being born did not keep the ties going by themselves. The time spent showing your face on others’ happy days and sad days had to pile up.

I cannot say for certain that I have put in enough of that time.

At that funeral, too, I was introduced as someone’s son.

The chief mourner recognized my father before my name.

But I was there.

On days of sorrow, people came before names.
